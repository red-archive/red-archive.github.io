ANNEX B // THE CHARGE SHEET AND THE DEFENCE // NUMBERS FIRST, ARGUMENT SECOND

THE LEDGER

The famines, the terror and the camps — what the archives actually hold, how the numbers moved when the archives opened, and the answers the communist tradition gives when the charge is put to it. The dead are not negotiated down here. Neither is the argument.

NUMBERS UNEDITED DEFENCE ENTERED IN FULL NO SÉANCES

01 The Charge Sheet, Unedited

Every argument about communism eventually arrives here, and an archive that flinches at this page has no business writing the other twenty. So the charge is entered first, at full weight, before a word of defence:

The Soviet famine, 1932–33

Excess deaths across the USSR are put at roughly 5.5 to 8.7 million, with Ukraine alone at 3.5 to 3.9 million and Kazakhstan losing something near a third of the Kazakh population — proportionally the worst-hit republic and the one least often named. See FILE 10.

The Great Terror, 1936–38

NKVD operational records list 681,692 executions in two years — roughly a thousand a day, sustained. Order No. 00447 set regional quotas for shooting and exile in advance. The Old Bolsheviks confessed to fabrications and were shot; the men who shot them were shot after.

The Gulag

Around 18 million passed through the camp system between 1930 and 1953, with 1.5 to 1.7 million documented deaths in custody — plus the special settlers, the deported nations, and those who died within weeks of release and so left the ledger clean.

The Great Leap Forward, 1959–61

Estimates run from 15 to 45 million excess deaths, the spread itself a scandal — Ó Gráda near 18m, Peng Xizhe 23m, Yang Jisheng 36m, Dikötter 45m. See FILE 12.

These are not contested by this archive and are not softened anywhere below. What follows is not a rebuttal of the deaths. It is the argument about what produced them — which is a different question, and the only one historians are actually still fighting about.

02 What the Archives Changed

The single most important fact about this subject is rarely mentioned in the arguments that invoke it: the numbers in circulation today are not the numbers of the Cold War, and they moved because the Soviet archives opened, not because anyone was persuaded.

Before 1991, the standard Western figures were extrapolations — demographic reconstruction, émigré testimony and defector accounts, assembled without access to a single Soviet personnel file. Robert Conquest's The Great Terror (1968) put the toll of the 1930s at around 20 million. Solzhenitsyn's Gulag Archipelago circulated figures as high as 60 million. Those numbers built the popular picture and are still quoted in it.

Then the files opened. In 1993, J. Arch Getty, Gábor Rittersporn and Viktor Zemskov published the NKVD's own internal accounting in the American Historical Review — the camp population registers, the execution tallies, the mortality returns kept by the institution for its own use. The documented figures came in several times lower than the Cold War estimates and several times higher than any Soviet public admission. Conquest, to his credit, revised in later editions.

"We now know, from the archives, roughly how many were shot, and roughly how many died in the camps. The revision is large. It is also not an acquittal — 681,692 executions in two years is a state killing its own citizens at industrial scale." — THE ARCHIVE, ON THE 1993 GETTY–RITTERSPORN–ZEMSKOV FIGURES

Both sides of the modern argument tend to misuse this. Anti-communists quote pre-archival numbers that the archives contradicted. Communists quote the revision as though a reduction from a wrong number to a documented one were a verdict of innocence. The archive's position is the boring one: use the documented figures, and note that they are still catastrophic.

03 The Famine Argument: Produced or Designed?

Nobody serious disputes that millions starved in 1932–33. The entire scholarly fight is over intent — whether the famine was manufactured to kill a nation, or produced by a policy that was pursued past the point of catastrophe. The distinction is the difference between a genocide and a crime of a different order, and it is genuinely unresolved.

The case for design

Ukraine-specific measures are the core of it: the January 1933 directive sealing the republic's borders against peasants leaving to find food; village blacklists cut off from all goods; the simultaneous assault on Ukrainian-language institutions and cadres. Timothy Snyder (Bloodlands, 2010) and Anne Applebaum (Red Famine, 2017) argue these turned a general famine into a national one. Raphael Lemkin, who coined the word genocide, applied it to Ukraine in 1953. More than thirty states and the European Parliament (2022) now formally recognise the Holodomor as genocide.

The case for production

R. W. Davies and Stephen Wheatcroft's The Years of Hunger (2004) — the most detailed archival study of the famine in any language — concluded that the catastrophe followed from procurement targets set on inflated harvest estimates, the chaos of forced collectivisation, and a genuinely poor 1932 harvest, and that the leadership reduced quotas and shipped relief once the scale became undeniable, too late and too little. Their verdict on Stalin is damning but specific: reckless, ideologically blinkered, criminally slow — not engaged in a plan to exterminate Ukrainians. Mark Tauger's harvest work supports the smaller-crop finding.

Note what the second position is not: it is not a denial, and Davies and Wheatcroft are not communists. It is the argument that a state can kill millions through doctrine, arrogance and a refusal to see, without a plan to do it — and that this is a description of a crime, not an excuse for one. Kazakhstan is the strongest evidence for it: the proportionally worse catastrophe, in a republic with no Ukrainian national question at all.

04 The Great Leap

China's famine of 1959–61 is the largest in recorded human history, and its estimate range is the widest of any modern demographic event. That range is not sloppiness — it reflects a genuine dispute over baseline mortality, census reconstruction and what counts as an excess death in a country whose normal death rate was already catastrophic by rich-world standards.

The mechanism is not seriously disputed: backyard steel furnaces pulling labour out of the harvest; close-planting and deep-ploughing directives issued as science; grain procurement set from wildly inflated local reports that cadres competed to exaggerate; and, decisively, the Lushan plenum of 1959 — where Peng Dehuai's letter describing the real conditions was treated as a factional attack, Peng was purged, and every official below him learned that reporting the famine was more dangerous than the famine. See FILE 12.

"The party's own 1981 resolution settled it in the only formula a ruling party could survive: Mao was seventy per cent correct and thirty per cent wrong. It is a political arithmetic, not a historical one — but it is an admission, entered by the prosecution's own successor." — ON THE CCP CENTRAL COMMITTEE'S RESOLUTION ON PARTY HISTORY, 1981

The defence offered from within the tradition has two halves. The first is that the Leap was a policy catastrophe rather than a campaign of killing — a distinction the CCP itself concedes on the record, which is more than most states manage about their own dead. The second is the one below, and it is the strongest card the tradition holds.

05 The Comparative Argument

This is where the communist case stops being defensive, and it deserves to be stated at full strength because it is empirically serious and rarely answered.

Amartya Sen — a liberal economist, a Nobel laureate, and no friend of Maoism — made the comparison that will not go away. India and China began at roughly the same appalling baseline in 1949. China then suffered the worst famine in history. India suffered none, because, as Sen argues, functioning democracies with a free press do not have famines. And yet, running the mortality tables forward across the whole period, Sen and Jean Drèze calculated that India's ordinary, unremarked, non-famine excess mortality — the routine deaths of the poor from hunger and preventable disease, year after year with no emergency to name them — exceeded China's famine deaths, and did so roughly every eight years.

"India seems to manage to fill its cupboard with more skeletons every eight years than China put there in its years of shame." — AMARTYA SEN & JEAN DRÈZE, HUNGER AND PUBLIC ACTION (1989)

The rest of the comparative ledger runs the same direction. Soviet life expectancy rose from the low forties at the end of the civil war to near seventy by the mid-1960s — the fastest sustained gain any large country had then recorded. Chinese life expectancy went from roughly 35 in 1949 to about 65 by 1980, and literacy from around a fifth of the population to two-thirds, across exactly the decades that contain the famine. Both states industrialised from peasant societies in a generation, under blockade, invasion or both.

The argument the tradition builds from this is not "the deaths were worth it." It is a challenge to the terms of the accounting: that capitalism's dead are counted as weather — Bengal 1943 under British administration, the Congo Free State, the Irish famine conducted under food export, the ongoing mortality of poverty in states nobody indexes — while socialism's dead are counted as ideology. Compared to what is a legitimate historical question, and the archive files it as one.

The strongest reply, entered here in full: a comparison is not an acquittal. That Britain starved Bengal does not un-starve Ukraine, and "the other system also kills" is an argument a communist would refuse instantly if a liberal made it. Sen's own conclusion was not that China was vindicated — it was that both mortality regimes were indictments, and that the free press is what makes the difference. The archive files that reply at equal weight.

06 What the Schools Themselves Said

The most-suppressed fact in this argument is that the fiercest prosecutions of Soviet and Chinese crimes were written by communists, in real time, at the cost of their lives. The tradition did not need to be told. It split over it.

Trotskyism. The Revolution Betrayed (1936) called the Soviet state a workers' state degenerated under a bureaucratic caste, and demanded a political revolution to break it. The diagnosis was sharp and the trials were frame-ups. What followed is the reason this archive files the school as a critic rather than a contender: eighty-six years, no state, no mass party, permanent schism — and a state-capitalist wing that supplied the American Cold War with Burnham, Shachtman and Kristol. The full rebuttal is in FILE 09 §07.

Left communism. Bordiga, Pannekoek and the council communists had reached the verdict earlier and gone further: what existed in the USSR was state capitalism — the wage relation intact, the surplus extracted by a state instead of a shareholder. On that reading the famines are not socialism's crimes at all; they are accumulation's, wearing a red flag.

Anarcho-communism. Kronstadt 1921 and the crushing of the Makhnovshchina were the anarchist indictment, entered a decade before the famine and never withdrawn. Goldman and Berkman published their disillusion and were denounced across the international left for it. They were right early, which is the expensive way to be right.

Maoism. Held Stalin at seventy per cent, blamed the restoration on Khrushchev's revisionism, and then produced its own catastrophe on the same logic. Hoxhaism defended Stalin without qualification and denounced Mao and Khrushchev alike as traitors — the purest position, held in the smallest country, at the price of total isolation.

Eurocommunism. After the tanks in Prague in 1968, the Western parties made the break public — Berlinguer's strappo, Carrillo's Eurocommunism and the State (1977) — and accepted parliamentary democracy permanently. Their critics upstairs note what the acceptance cost them: within fifteen years most of these parties had dissolved into social democracy and stopped being communist at all.

So "communism never reckoned with its dead" is false on the documentary record. It reckoned so violently that it fractured into the twenty files this archive is made of. Every schism in the conflicts file is, at bottom, an argument about this page.

07 What Survives the Argument

The archive's method is to say which defences hold and which do not, and to say it plainly.

Holds

That the Cold War figures were inflated and the archives corrected them downward. That famine causation is genuinely contested among non-communist historians, and intentionality in Ukraine is not a settled fact. That the comparative mortality question is legitimate and almost never asked in the other direction. That the tradition prosecuted its own crimes before its enemies did.

Does not hold

That the famines did not happen, or that the documented figures are fabrications. That the Terror was a defensive necessity — the quotas were set in advance, on paper. That another system's crimes retire these. That a state which shoots its own founding generation can be defended by anyone who claims the workers should hold power.

What the twenty files upstairs actually record is a movement that took power in the poorest countries on earth, industrialised them at a speed nothing else has matched, beat fascism at the cost of twenty-seven million of its own, and built a machinery of repression that consumed its own revolution. Both halves are true simultaneously. Any account that needs one of them deleted is propaganda — and the archive has no interest in producing propaganda for anybody, including for the side it is most often accused of serving.
