01 Origin Vector

Mao Zedong — Hunan farmer's son, library assistant, founding member of the CCP in 1921 — made his heresy early. Moscow's blueprint said organize the urban proletariat; China's cities answered in 1927, when Chiang Kai-shek massacred the communists in Shanghai. Mao's Report on the Peasant Movement in Hunan had already drawn the other conclusion: the revolutionary class was in the fields — "a single spark can start a prairie fire." The party fled to the mountains, built the Jiangxi Soviet, survived encirclement, and in 1934–35 walked 9,000 kilometres under fire to Shaanxi. Of roughly 86,000 who began the Long March, some 8,000 arrived. En route, at the Zunyi Conference, Mao took effective command of the party; he never relinquished it.

From the cave city of Yan'an, Mao spent a decade writing the doctrine (On Contradiction, On Practice, On Protracted War) while his armies grew inside the Japanese occupation. By 1949 the strategy Moscow had called impossible had taken the mainland: "the Chinese people have stood up." The archive notes the operative word — the school's entire subsequent history turns on who counts as the people, and who decides.

02 Core Doctrine

The peasantry as revolutionary class

The inversion that founds the file: in the colonized and semi-feudal world, the countryside is not backup — it is the main force. Revolution's center of gravity moves from Europe's factories to the global South's villages, permanently.

Protracted people's war

Strategy in three stages: defensive, stalemate, offensive. Build liberated base areas, win the peasantry ("the people are the sea, the army the fish"), encircle the cities over decades. Time is a weapon; the enemy's impatience is a supply line.

The mass line

"From the masses, to the masses": gather the people's scattered ideas, systematize, return them as policy, test, repeat. Leadership as feedback loop rather than command — in doctrine; the archive cross-references practice below.

Contradiction, ongoing

On Contradiction: everything develops through internal opposites; distinguish antagonistic from non-antagonistic contradictions and handle each accordingly. Crucially: contradictions — including class struggle — continue under socialism, inside the party itself. The theoretical license for everything after 1962.

Continuous revolution

The revolution that stops, restores. Against Soviet "revisionism," the masses must periodically bombard their own headquarters lest the party harden into a new ruling class. The Cultural Revolution is this card played on a civilization.

New Democracy → socialism

The staged theory: first a broad anti-imperialist coalition under party leadership, then socialist transformation. Every subsequent Maoist party carries this two-stage map into its own national terrain.

04 In Practice

In power, the school ran the century's largest experiments and its ledger is proportionate. Land reform broke the landlord class (with mass killings attached); literacy and barefoot-doctor campaigns transformed village life; life expectancy roughly doubled across the Mao era. Against this: the Great Leap Forward (1958–62) — communes, backyard steel, fabricated harvest reports — produced the deadliest famine in recorded history — the estimate range, the mechanism and the argument are filed in Annex B — with scholarly estimates running from fifteen to forty-five million dead. Retreating from its wreckage, Mao answered his own party's verdict with the Cultural Revolution (1966–76): Red Guards against "capitalist roaders," the presidency liquidated, schools closed, perhaps a million or more dead and a civilization's inheritance smashed as "the Four Olds." His designated heir Lin Biao — compiler of the Little Red Book — died in 1971 fleeing by air after an alleged coup plot; the faithful were left to parse the omen.

Abroad, the doctrine became the global South's revolutionary lingua franca: Vietnam adapted it, Cambodia's Khmer Rouge — the file's most monstrous pupil — reduced it to year-zero terror, and after Mao's death the systematized export, Marxism–Leninism–Maoism (Peru's Gonzalo, 1980s), armed people's wars in Peru, Nepal and India. Nepal's Maoists fought to a peace deal and won elections; the Naxalite corridor still burns. Maoism is the only school in this archive with active front lines.

05 Schisms & Enemies

  • vs. Moscow (1956–1969). De-Stalinization as betrayal, peaceful coexistence as capitulation — Schism №10. Ends in border artillery and Nixon's handshake.
  • vs. Hoxha (1972–1978). The Three Worlds Theory and the American opening cost Mao his last European ally — Schism №11.
  • vs. Deng (1976–1981). The in-house repudiation — Schism №13: "70% correct, 30% wrong," the Cultural Revolution condemned, the portrait retained.
  • Internal, armed. The Cultural Revolution was a schism conducted with artillery inside one party: Liu Shaoqi died in a cell, Deng was purged twice, Lin Biao fell from the sky. The school's doctrine of struggle inside the party was honored, comprehensively.

07 Legacy

Maoism globalized communism: it broke the doctrine's European monopoly, made the village a revolutionary headquarters, and handed every colonized country a strategy manual that demonstrably worked once. Its governing record contains both the century's largest famine and its largest gains in peasant life expectancy — the archive keeps both cards in the same drawer. In China its heir is FILE 20, which quotes it on holidays and jails its orthodox readers; in the hills of India its heirs still carry rifles. No other file is simultaneously a state religion, a live insurgency, and a museum exhibit.
