ORTHODOX MARXISM

01 Origin Vector

When Engels died in 1895, the keys to the doctrine passed to Karl Kautsky — "the pope of Marxism" — and the institutional church was already built: the Second International (founded 1889) and its flagship, the German SPD, the largest, richest, best-organized workers' party on earth. A million members; ninety daily papers; its own schools, gyms, choirs, funeral societies — a complete counter-society waiting, in theory, to inherit.

The doctrine Kautsky codified in the Erfurt Program (1891) held the two halves together with a comma: capitalism's concentration and crises make socialism inevitable (the revolutionary maximum program), and until then the party fights for reforms (the minimum program). The SPD, in August Bebel's formula, was "a revolutionary party, but not a party that makes revolutions." History would do the heavy lifting; the party's job was to be ready.

02 Core Doctrine

Marxism as science

Historical materialism read as natural law: capitalism's breakdown is a prediction on par with astronomy. Kautsky's generation, raised on Darwin, treated the revolution as an evolutionary certainty — which quietly made insurrection look unscientific.

The party as counter-world

Organize everything: press, schools, unions, cooperatives, youth leagues. The class becomes conscious inside the party's institutions, and the vote registers the accumulating majority year by year.

Minimum / maximum program

Reforms now, revolution eventually — the Erfurt architecture. Its critics on both wings noticed the same thing: the comma between the programs carried the whole structure, and nobody could say when "eventually" arrived.

Attentisme

The strategy of revolutionary waiting. Do not gamble the organization on adventures; capitalism is doing the work. Luxemburg called this the "parliamentary cretinism" of men who would keep the party safe until it was irrelevant.

"The Social Democratic Party is a revolutionary party, but not a party that makes revolutions."— KARL KAUTSKY, THE ROAD TO POWER ERA

04 In Practice

For twenty-five years the model worked exactly as designed: the SPD's vote climbed election after election until, in 1912, it was the largest party in the Reichstag. Then came the design's single untested assumption. On 4 August 1914 the parliamentary fraction voted unanimously for the Kaiser's war credits, and the International's parties followed their own flags into the trenches. The revolutionary counter-society turned out to be, at the decisive hour, a patriotic one.

The church's afterlife was governmental: the SPD took power in the 1918 German Revolution — and used Freikorps mercenaries to crush its own left, killing Luxemburg and Liebknecht. Orthodoxy's two heirs were now irreconcilable: a social democracy that governed capitalism, and a communism that never forgave it. Kautsky spent his last two decades writing against both Bolshevism and fascism, and died in Amsterdam exile in 1938, the year the purges consumed what remained of his Russian students.

05 Schisms & Enemies

  • The revisionism debate (1896–1903). Bernstein audited the prophecy — wages rising, middle class not vanishing — and proposed dropping the collapse: "the movement is everything." Kautsky and Luxemburg condemned him formally; the party kept his practice informally. Schism №2.
  • August 1914. The war-credits vote — Schism №4 — killed the Second International as a revolutionary body and split every member party into patriots and internationalists.
  • vs. Lenin (1918). Kautsky's The Dictatorship of the Proletariat called the Bolshevik state socialism without democracy, therefore not socialism; Lenin's reply made "renegade" a permanent title. Democratic socialism and communism date their divorce from this exchange — Schism №5.

07 Legacy

Orthodox Marxism is the trunk from which the twentieth century's two great rivals both split: social democracy kept its practice and dropped the revolution; Leninism kept the revolution and dropped the patience. Its buildings — the mass membership party, the program, the party school — furnished every successor. Its cautionary lesson is sharper: a theory of inevitable victory is also a license for infinite postponement, and an institution built to inherit the future will, at the decisive hour, protect the institution.
