01 Origin Vector

The school was built from funerals. 1956, Budapest: Western parties defend the tanks and bleed members for a decade. 1968, Prague: the tanks again, but this time Italy's PCI and Spain's PCE condemn them publicly — the first crack in bloc discipline that Moscow cannot purge, because these parties elect real deputies with real votes. 1973, Santiago: Allende's democratic road ends in Pinochet's coup, and Enrico Berlinguer draws the school's founding lesson in three essays: a left that wins 51% can still be destroyed if it terrifies the other 49 — therefore the "historic compromise," an alliance reaching to Christian Democracy itself, and democracy not as a road to socialism but as its content.

By the mid-1970s the tendency had a name (coined by a journalist, adopted under protest), a summit (Madrid 1977: Berlinguer, Santiago Carrillo, Georges Marchais), and a manifesto — Carrillo's "Eurocommunism" and the State (1977), which said the quiet part in print: the Soviet state was not a workers' democracy but something closer to its opposite, and the October model was "no longer valid" for advanced countries. Moscow's New Times answered with a formal anathema. The Western parties had, at last, their own schism — Schism №12 — and for once they were the ones doing the leaving.

02 Core Doctrine

Democracy as content, not vehicle

Pluralism, elections, alternation in power, civil liberties: accepted permanently, not tactically — socialism will be reached through them or will not be worth reaching. The explicit repudiation of the dictatorship of the proletariat, deleted from party programs in so many words.

Polycentrism

Togliatti's 1956 seed: no single center of world communism, no Rome and no third Rome. Each party answers to its national terrain — the Titoist heresy generalized to parties that had never held power.

The historic compromise

Berlinguer's Chilean lesson: advanced societies cannot be governed against their middle classes. Build the broadest democratic bloc — including the Catholic world — and transform hegemonically, Gramsci's war of position run through a parliamentary majority.

Under NATO's umbrella

The scandal clause: Berlinguer told an interviewer in 1976 he felt "safer" building socialism inside NATO than outside it — the shield against the fate of Prague being, precisely, the Atlantic alliance. Moscow filed the sentence as treason; the archive files it as the school's entire geometry in one line.

"The October Revolution model no longer serves for the working-class movement in the developed capitalist countries."— SANTIAGO CARRILLO, "EUROCOMMUNISM" AND THE STATE (1977)

04 In Practice

The high tide was Italian: in June 1976 the PCI took 34.4% — communism's best free-election result anywhere, ever — and entered the parliamentary majority's supporting perimeter. What followed was the school's crucible: the "years of lead," and in 1978 the Red Brigades' kidnapping and murder of Aldo Moro, the Christian Democrat architect of the opening to the PCI. The historic compromise died with him; the PCI got austerity's bills without government's levers, and began the long slide. In Spain, Carrillo's PCE traded revolutionary claims for legalization and co-wrote the democratic transition — the school's most concrete achievement, and the party's electoral self-erasure. France's PCF, always the reluctant third, endorsed the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in 1980 and went home to Moscow's orbit, resolving its Eurocommunism as a lapsed subscription.

Berlinguer pressed on — condemning martial law in Poland in 1981 with the declaration that October's "propulsive force" was exhausted — and died mid-speech, mid-campaign, in 1984; a million and a half people attended the funeral, the school's last mass mobilization. When 1989 removed the question, the PCI dissolved itself into a democratic-left party with two years' notice. The tendency's obituaries divide the archive: it either proved communism could democratize itself — or proved that when it does, it stops being distinguishable, then stops being.

05 Schisms & Enemies

  • vs. Moscow (1975–1981). The formal divorce — Schism №12: anathemas over Carrillo, fury over NATO, final rupture over Afghanistan and Poland. The CPSU's last excommunication of a living movement.
  • vs. the armed left. The Red Brigades aimed at exactly the compromise Berlinguer built; the PCI answered with the "party of order" — expelling, denouncing, and losing its own left flank to the autonomia it helped prosecute.
  • vs. its own arithmetic. The unresolved schism was with the strategy itself: each step toward the center traded identity for respectability, and the voters eventually took the respectability from the original vendors — the Socialists. The school never found the answer; its successors are still billing hours on the question.

07 Legacy

Eurocommunism is the archive's test of the oldest question — reform or revolution — run at full scale with a real third of a real electorate. It delivered concrete goods: Spain's peaceful transition, the final proof that communist parties could break with Moscow and survive, and the grammar every democratic-socialist revival since has spoken, usually without citation. Its cautionary clause is the mirror of Hoxha's: where FILE 17 shows what holding every line costs, this file shows what surrendering them buys — governance's neighborhood, never its house, and a name that dissolved on contact with its own success. The archive shelves them facing each other, as intended.
