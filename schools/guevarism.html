GUEVARISM

The foco: don't wait for revolutionary conditions — a small armed nucleus in the mountains creates them. Revolutionary will over revolutionary patience, tested on three continents, vindicated once.

01 Origin Vector

Ernesto Guevara — Argentine, asthmatic, medical doctor — was radicalized on a motorcycle: the 1952 journey up the continent's spine showed him the mines and plantations, and Guatemala 1954 showed him the CIA removing an elected government that touched United Fruit's land. The lesson he took to his grave: reform invites the coup; only armed power protects the revolution. In Mexico City he met Fidel Castro's exiles and boarded the yacht Granma as expedition doctor. Of eighty-two who landed in Cuba in December 1956, about a dozen effectives regrouped in the Sierra Maestra. Twenty-five months later they were in Havana.

That arithmetic — twelve men to a state in two years — became the doctrine. Che's Guerrilla Warfare (1960) and Régis Debray's systematization Revolution in the Revolution? (1967) distilled it: the insurrectionary foco (focus) does not wait for the party, the proletariat or the objective conditions. The guerrilla band is "the party in embryo"; action is propaganda; the countryside is the arena. It was Leninism with the waiting deleted — and, critics noted immediately, with Cuba's exceptional circumstances promoted to universal law.

02 Core Doctrine

The foco

A small, mobile armed nucleus rooted in rough terrain. It need not reflect existing discontent; by fighting and surviving it demonstrates the regime's mortality and detonates the latent revolution. The insurrection precedes the party — Leninism inverted.

Conditions are made, not awaited

Against every patient school in this archive: "it is not necessary to wait until all conditions for making revolution exist; the insurrection can create them." The sentence over which Guevarism and orthodoxy permanently parted.

Armed propaganda

Every ambush is a communiqué. The guerrilla's real target is consciousness: each survival proves the army is not invincible, each reprisal recruits the village it punishes.

The New Man

Socialism and Man in Cuba (1965): socialism must produce a person moved by moral rather than material incentives — voluntary labour, internationalist duty. Che worked the cane fields on Sundays as advertisement. The doctrine's gentlest text, guarding its hardest core.

Tricontinentalism

"Create two, three, many Vietnams": imperialism is a world system and must be bled at its peripheries simultaneously. Havana's 1966 Tricontinental conference made the slogan institutional.

"Wherever death may surprise us, let it be welcome, provided that this, our battle cry, may have reached some receptive ear."— CHE GUEVARA, MESSAGE TO THE TRICONTINENTAL (1967)

04 In Practice

The doctrine's record is Cuba, then the bill. In victorious Havana, Che ran the National Bank (signing the currency "Che") and the Industry Ministry, presided over revolutionary tribunals at La Cabaña — the file does not omit them — and grew impatient with both superpowers: his 1965 Algiers speech accused the socialist bloc itself of trading like imperialists. Then he vanished from public view and re-ran the experiment twice. In the Congo (1965): failure, recorded in his own unsparing diary. In Bolivia (1966–67): the peasants did not rise, the Communist Party withheld support, the CIA-trained Rangers closed the ring. Captured at Quebrada del Yuro on 8 October 1967, he was shot the next day in the Vallegrande schoolhouse. The army displayed the body; the photograph — eyes open, faintly smiling — did more recruiting than the foco ever had.

Imitator focos failed across the continent through the 1960s; Marighella moved the method to the cities and died there; the one arguable later success, Nicaragua's Sandinistas (1979), won by mass insurrection — the strategy the foco was designed to skip. Debray, the doctrine's own systematizer, recanted from a Bolivian prison. The verdict most cited in the archive: Cuba proved what a foco can do against a rotten, isolated dictatorship; everywhere else proved how rare that conjunction is.

05 Schisms & Enemies

  • vs. the orthodox communist parties. Latin America's Moscow-line parties preferred elections and alliances; Che's shorthand for them was unprintable. Bolivia's party, decisively, sat out his last campaign. The quarrel — vanguard of arms versus vanguard of patience — killed focos from Guatemala to Peru.
  • vs. peaceful coexistence. The Algiers speech burned the school's bridges to both Moscow and, awkwardly, its own patron in Havana: solidarity, Che held, is not a trade policy.
  • vs. Allende's ballot road, posthumously. Chile 1973 became the school's grim vindication in absentia: the coup Che had predicted for any unarmed socialism arrived on schedule. Eurocommunism (FILE 19) drew the opposite lesson from the same corpse — the archive shelves the two readings facing each other.

07 Legacy

Guevarism contributed the archive's most portable article of faith — that revolution is an act of will — and its most reproduced image; Korda's photograph has sold more units than any argument in this building. The doctrine proper is studied mainly as cautionary strategy, its casualty list running from Bolivia's canyons to a generation of Latin American militants. What survives intact is the temperament: the refusal to wait, the internationalism without portfolio, and the standing rebuke — priced at one life, paid in full — to every school that filed revolution under "eventually."
