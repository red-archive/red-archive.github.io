THE PLATE ROOM // ART, POSTERS AND MOVING AGITPROP

THE PLATE ROOM

The visual record: monuments, posters, murals and the moving agitprop the movement makes about itself. The files upstairs argue in prose. This room is what the argument looks like when it is trying to move people rather than convince them, which the tradition has always understood to be a different craft.

01 Plates

02 The Soviet Wall

State poster art from the first Soviet decades, when the medium was doing work no other medium could: a population that was majority illiterate in 1917 was being addressed by a government that needed it to understand a programme. The results are not decoration. Each of these is an argument from one of the twenty files upstairs, cut down until it could be read at a distance by someone who could not read.

The interesting thing about the sequence is how quickly it turns. The 1919 and 1925 plates argue for a world revolution; by 1929 the same presses are arguing for a production target, and by 1930 they are producing a face. Annex B covers what the politics of that turn cost.

03 The Chinese Wall

The Chinese material sits differently, because the Cultural Revolution's characteristic medium was not the printed state poster but the dazibao — the big-character poster, handwritten, put up by anyone, and aimed at a named person. It is the only mass propaganda form in this archive that ordinary people produced themselves rather than received, which is exactly what made it uncontrollable. FILE 12 files the consequences; Annex F files the book that travelled beside it.

04 Moving Agitprop

Short-form edits made and circulated by the movement's own accounts. The archive files them as artefacts of how the tradition presents itself now, in the medium it actually reaches people through, which is the same reason FILE 10 filed the poster and the newsreel.

05 Provenance

The clips in §02 were made and posted by other people. They are reproduced here as archive material, unaltered except for re-encoding to a smaller file size, and the archive claims no authorship of any of them. If you made one of these and want it removed or credited differently, the repository is public and an issue or a message will do it — see the source.

Plates in §01 are a mix of photographs of public monuments, historical posters and contemporary murals. The portraits used throughout the twenty files upstairs are hotlinked from Wikimedia Commons under their own licences and are not stored here.

The Soviet and Chinese walls in §02 and §03 are vendored from Wikimedia Commons and re-encoded down to web size. Every one is public domain, CC0 or CC-licensed; the CC BY and CC BY-SA files carry an attribution requirement, so the credit is printed here rather than buried in the repository. Each title links to the Commons file page and its full licence terms.

A note on the Chinese material, since the archive would rather state a limit than pretend it does not exist: most People's Republic poster art from the 1950s to the 1970s is still in copyright — the PRC term is the life of the author plus fifty years, and many of those artists died after 1976. What is reproduced above is the subset that is genuinely free: press photographs and documents that fall under the PRC's copyright exemptions, and scans released under CC licences. The famous poster art of the period is deliberately absent, and its absence is a licensing fact rather than an editorial judgement.
